# Investment Thesis
In my last article on Nvidia Corporation (NVDA), published in March 2026, I analyzed the company’s Q4 report and explored the key takeaways. My thesis was predominantly focused on the surge in supply commitments that was announced by the company and the impact these commitments would have on the long-term growth of NVDA. I also examined the intensity of the competitive environment and whether investors had reasons to worry. I had a Strong Buy rating on the stock.

Since my article was published, the stock has gained 17.1%, comfortably outperforming the S&P 500 (SP500), which gained 9.6% during the same period.

In this article, I analyze the impact of the company’s Q1 FY27 earnings report, with a special focus on how the company’s long-term growth story has evolved since my last article. More specifically, while my CUDA thesis from my last article remains intact, I argue that the more consequential takeaway from the Q1 report is the company’s expansion into the data center and client CPU markets – a multi-hundred-billion dollar potential that the market, in my view, is yet to price in. I also weigh my thesis against the intensifying threat from the custom silicon players and argue why, despite the stock’s multiple de-ratings in recent times, the risk-reward continues to remain favorable for long-term investors.

Before delving into the thesis, I have to mention a brief note on the methodology that would be used in the valuation section later. Most of my recent articles have deployed machine-learning ensembles to project forward EPS. For NVDA, the approach was the same. However, the framework could not reliably forecast NVDA’s forward EPS. This does not mean that all the ensemble models used in my previous articles failed. This is an NVDA-specific issue; more specifically, NVDA’s current earnings regime, which is predominantly driven by a data-center AI demand shock that occurred back in late 2022 and accelerated through 2023, sits outside anything in the four decades of semiconductor history that the model was trained on.

Put simply, the company’s EPS trajectory today is out-of-distribution relative to the data on which the model was trained. The reliability issue could not be resolved even after adding the single most important demand variable in my view, the hyperscaler capex, which confirms that the issue is structural. Therefore, I have valued NVDA structurally in this article, using a scenario-based approach, and I treat the machine-learning ensemble only as an out-of-distribution cross-check.

# NVDA’s Q1 Print Quietly Reshaped the Demand Narrative
It was a strong quarterly performance from NVDA. While every headline metric was strong, the more important signal, in my opinion, was the structural shift seen in the demand composition. More specifically, $37.9 billion of the $75.2 billion in data center revenues were accounted for by hyperscalers, with the remaining attributed to the ACIE bucket, which comprises sovereign AI, enterprise on-premise deployment, and AI cloud providers. Sovereign AI alone grew more than 80% y/y, and AI cloud revenues more than tripled over the same period. The nearly equal split between the two data center segments is a departure from the norm, which was that hyperscalers accounted for well over 50% of the data center revenues, and it indicates that NVDA’s demand base is beginning to diversify. This implies that the customer concentration risk, which was a key argument of NVDA bears, is reducing.

This is not to say the impact of hyperscaler capex is fading away. Four of the largest AI spenders – Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Meta Platforms (META), and Amazon (AMZN) – have collectively committed to spending approximately $700 billion in 2026. In Q1, the aggregate capex from the four tech giants grew around 80% y/y, and the spending is showing no signs of slowing down.

Taken together, the continued acceleration in the capex spending of NVDA’s top customers and the beginning of data center revenue diversification keep the long-term growth story of NVDA firmly intact.

# The Vera CPU Gives NVDA Keys to a Potential $200 Billion Kingdom
The most significant development since my last article, one that has recently started gaining a lot more attention, is NVDA’s entry into the CPU market. This represents a fundamentally different growth catalyst compared to NVDA’s GPU franchise.

During the Q1 earnings call, CEO Jensen Huang stated that the company already has a visibility of nearly $20 billion in standalone Vera CPU revenues for FY27, which represents nearly 10% of a potential $200 billion total addressable market. The revenue figure is on top of the revenues expected to be gained from the Vera CPU that are shipped as part of the integrated Vera Rubin systems. CFO Collette Kress argued that the Vera CPU has opened up a “brand new $200 billion tab” for the company and highlighted the company’s ambitions of becoming the world’s leading CPU supplier. The Vera CPU is purpose-built for agentic AI and offers nearly 1.5x more performance per core, 2x the performance per watt, and 4x the rack density compared to the x86 processors.

The broader CPU market is experiencing a resurgence as a result of the structural shift from call-and-response chatbots to agentic, task-oriented AI, as a result of which a significant number of companies are migrating from x86 to Arm-based architectures. NVDA’s entry into this space, therefore, is well-timed, in my opinion.

Furthermore, at Computex, NVDA unveiled the RTX Spark, an Arm-based superchip for laptops and desktops with major OEM partners. The RTX Spark is set to be launched in the fall of this year. While the initial positioning of the RTX Spark is premium and niche, the launch still presents NVDA with a runway beyond FY28 into the resurgent client-CPU market.

Taken together, the combination of NVDA’s attack on the resurgent CPU market is a major growth catalyst, one that the current valuation is not reflecting.

As mentioned earlier, a bottom-up structural forecast model is used to value NVDA since the machine-learning ensemble model was found to be out-of-distribution for the company’s current earnings regime. The valuation process is scenario-based and is built on explicit assumptions on revenues and margins, as well as the forward P/E multiple.

NVDA reported $81.6 billion in Q1 FY27 and has projected revenues to come in at $91.0 billion in Q2 [excluding revenues from China]. I have assumed a sequential growth of 9% in Q3 revenues and 10% in Q4 revenues – strong but decelerating compared to recent quarters [the step-up in Q4 is on account of the ramp-up in Vera Rubin production]. This results in FY27 revenues of $381 billion. At the company’s guided gross margins of approximately 75%, and assuming operating expenses of $35 billion and a 17% tax rate, results in an FY27 EPS of approximately $8.47.

For FY28, I assumed revenue growth of 30% in my base, taking into account a full year of contributions from Vera Rubin as well as a ramp in Vera CPU, and offset by continued market share erosion to custom silicon and no revenues from China. Assuming gross margins are maintained at 75%, operating expenses of $42 billion, and a tax rate of 17%, the base case FY28 EPS comes in at $11.12.

NVDA currently trades at a forward P/E multiple of 23.3x, according to Seeking Alpha, significantly below its Mag Seven peer average of about 28x, and an even further discount to its direct peers such as Broadcom (AVGO) and TSMC (TSM), which trade closer to 37-39x. It is important to note here that my price targets apply scenario multiples to FY28 EPS, which is consistent with twelve-month targets.

In my opinion, given the catalysts outlined in this article and given the consistent strong growth delivered by the company so far, there is no growth-based justification for NVDA to trade below its slower-growing customers such as MSFT and AMZN. More importantly, NVDA is the supplier, who is capturing the bulk of these customers’ capex, and it is managing to grow earnings considerably faster, making it the cheapest high-quality name, on a PEG basis, in the group.

The discount could be attributed to the custom-silicon threat, which is real, but based on my thesis, the discount is overdone simply because the market is currently ignoring both the diversification of NVDA’s demand base and its offense when it comes to the CPU market. I have therefore anchored my base-case multiple at 28x, the Mag Seven average, since the thesis supports my view that NVDA deserves to trade at least in line with its customers. For the bear case, my forward P/E is anchored at 23x, essentially holding the current multiple so that even under a scenario where the P/E discount persists, any modest upside comes entirely from earnings growth rather than any multiple re-rating. For the bull case, the forward P/E is anchored at 35x, a premium to the Mag 7 average but still marginally below NVDA’s direct semiconductor peers.

Taken together, I am maintaining my Strong Buy rating on NVDA, with a revised base-case price target of $311. My thesis has evolved from the CUDA moat, which still remains in my opinion, to data center revenue diversification and an offensive strategy to tackle the resurgent CPU market. The base-case upside can now be attributed more to earnings growth rather than from multiple expansion alone.

# Risk Factors
The primary risk to my thesis is the potential accelerated erosion of NVDA’s market share in the inference market, especially if the hyperscalers’ own plans of vertical integration progresses at a faster pace than expected [the significant backlog of Broadcom and the economics of Trainium3 from AMZN makes this a realistic scenario]. This would pressurize both the revenue growth and the durability premium embedded in my base case multiple, causing the bear case to be the more realistic central case.

The second risk is related to margins. My base case has assumed that NVDA maintains its gross margins at approximately 75%. The ramp-up of Vera Rubin could potentially dilute the margins, especially at the early stages. The FY28 EPS in each of the scenarios would be lower than what has been currently modeled should the margin compression prove to be more persistent.

Finally, management’s guidance has already factored in the potential revenue loss from China. However, should there be any further tightening of export controls, especially related to other geographies, it would undermine the demand floor on which the model’s revenue assumptions rest.

# Concluding Thoughts

NVDA delivered yet another strong quarterly performance, beating on both the top and bottom lines, and data center revenues seeing further acceleration. More importantly, from a long-term perspective, the quarter revealed a demand base that is diversifying meaningfully from the hyperscalers. The aggressive expansion into the multi-hundred-billion-dollar CPU market through the Vera CPU and the RTX Spark opens up yet another powerful long-term growth catalyst.

While the market is rightfully pricing the threats to NVDA – the gradual market share erosion of inference share to custom silicon and a potential structural loss of China revenues – it is, however, materially underpricing the expansion of the company’s growth runway, in my opinion. The $200 billion total addressable market of Vera CPU and the diversification of demand into sovereign and enterprise channels, in my view, more than offset the increasing intensity of the competitive landscape. The fact that NVDA trades at a discount to the very customers whose capex funds its revenue captures this disconnect.

Overall, I continue to view NVDA as a high-quality franchise trading at a valuation that, in my view, does not reflect the breadth of its growth opportunity. I maintain my Strong Buy rating with a base-case price target of $311, and I continue to remain a long-term holder of the stock.
